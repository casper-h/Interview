# Behavioural Interview Prep

At the senior level and above, the behavioural round is frequently a hard gate, not a formality. It
is where interviewers assess ownership, judgment under ambiguity, and how you operate with other
people, none of which the coding or system design rounds directly test. Treat this track with the
same rigor as the technical ones, and note that this is the round where preparing to the Staff bar
pays off most directly. The scope, influence, and leadership stories that read as merely "good" for
a Senior candidate are exactly what pushes an evaluation from lean hire to strong hire.

## What's actually being evaluated

Across most top companies, behavioural interviewers are scoring some subset of the following.

- **Ownership and scope**: do you take responsibility beyond your ticket, and do you drive things
  to completion?
- **Judgment under ambiguity**: how do you make decisions with incomplete information?
- **Conflict and influence**: how do you handle disagreement, and how do you influence others
  without formal authority?
- **Failure and growth**: how do you respond when something goes wrong, and what did you change
  afterward?
- **Collaboration**: how do you work with PMs, designers, and other engineers across teams?
- **Communication**: can you tell a clear, structured, appropriately concise story?
- **Technical judgment**: trade-off reasoning shows up here too, not just in system design.

If you're targeting a specific company, it helps to map your stories to their language.

- **Amazon** grades explicitly against the Leadership Principles (Ownership, Bias for Action,
  Dive Deep, Disagree and Commit, Deliver Results, and others). Expect at least two dedicated
  behavioural rounds. Bias for Action is the most-probed principle after Ownership, so have a story
  where you acted on incomplete information and can defend why waiting for more data was the wrong
  call, without the story reading as reckless.
- **Google** looks for "Googleyness" (comfort with ambiguity, intellectual humility,
  conscientiousness, and being someone people want to work with), general cognitive ability signals,
  and leadership without authority. Prepare one story that shows intellectual humility (you were
  wrong, saw it, and changed course) alongside the scope stories.
- **Meta** evaluates along "Direction," "Execution," and "Talent," with heavy emphasis on impact
  and ambiguity. "Talent" is about growing and raising the bar for other engineers, so have a
  people-development story ready (rows 4 and 20 below), not only delivery stories.
- For most other companies, prepare for the general list above; it covers the common ground.

## The STAR framework, and how to not sound robotic doing it

- **Situation**: one or two sentences of context. Don't over-explain the org chart.
- **Task**: what specifically was your responsibility, or the problem you decided to own.
- **Action**: the majority of your answer. Use "I," not "we," when describing your specific
  contribution, and narrate your decisions and the reasoning behind them, not just a chronological
  list of steps.
- **Result**: quantify it if at all possible (percentage, dollar amount, time saved, people
  affected). Then add what you'd do differently or what you learned. This is often what separates
  a strong-hire answer from a merely adequate one.

Aim for 90 seconds to two and a half minutes per story unless the interviewer digs in with
follow-ups. Rehearse out loud, not just in your head; writing a story and being able to tell it
fluently under pressure are different skills.

## Handling follow-up probing

The initial STAR answer is the easy part. At the senior level and above, the signal lives in the
follow-ups, and a rehearsed monologue that can't survive probing reads as coached rather than lived.
Interviewers push on the *reasoning*, not the events. Prepare for these shapes on every story:

- **"Why did you choose that over the alternative?"** Have the option you rejected and the reason
  ready. "There wasn't an alternative" is almost never a believable answer for a real decision.
- **"What would you do differently, or what did you get wrong?"** Every strong story has an honest
  answer here. Refusing to name one is a red flag; naming a real one is a strong-hire signal.
- **"Who disagreed, and how did you handle it?"** Be ready to name the disagreement specifically,
  and to show you engaged with the other view rather than steamrolled it.
- **"What was the impact, and how do you know?"** Be able to defend your numbers: how they were
  measured, and what you'd say if the metric were questioned.
- **"What would change at ten times the scale / with half the time / if it failed?"** The
  counterfactual probe. It tests whether you understand *why* your decision worked, not just that it
  did.

The trap is over-rehearsing the wording until you can only recite the happy path. Rehearse the
*content and the reasoning* so you can answer an unexpected angle in your own words, not a script.

## Interview-type specifics

Not every behavioural conversation is the same round, and preparing them identically is a common
miss.

- **Peer / team-member round.** Tactical: how you actually work, collaborate, handle conflict, and
  make day-to-day technical calls. The story bank below is aimed squarely here.
- **Hiring-manager round.** More strategic, and often the final gate. Expect scope, impact, "why
  this team," and how you operate with a manager and handle direction and disagreement. This is
  where the row 11 to 20 scope stories and ["why this company, why now"](../resume_and_narrative/README.md)
  get stress-tested, along with forward-looking questions ("how would you approach X on this team?")
  that no rehearsed past-tense story covers. Prepare manager-facing questions of your own:
  team roadmap and challenges, how success is measured, and what growth from this level looks like.
- **Values / culture round.** Common as a final round or at values-driven companies. Map your
  stories to the company's stated values or leadership principles ahead of time, and expect probing
  on judgment calls where values traded off against each other (shipping vs quality, speed vs
  consensus).

## Story bank: build eight to ten stories that cover these situations

Aim for real projects, ideally from the last two or three years, ideally with quantifiable
impact. One story can often answer multiple question types, so tag each story against the
competencies it covers to make it easy to reach for in the moment.

The first ten cover the individual-contributor core. Rows 11 to 20 are the scope, influence, and
leadership stories that push an evaluation toward strong hire; they are the ones a Senior candidate
most often has a thin or missing answer for, and the ones a strong hire tells naturally. You do not
need a distinct story for all twenty, since one rich project often answers several rows at once (a
cross-team project may cover collaboration, a conflict, and a hard prioritization call), so tag each
story with every row it serves rather than manufacturing twenty separate ones. Prioritize building at
least three or four from rows 11 to 20, drawn from the highest-scope work you have actually done, and
weight toward the higher-stakes ones: a story where a wrong call cost real money, users, or team
health reads as stronger scope than a clean win on a small surface. If your current scope hasn't
reached one of these, the honest version (how you drove the largest slice you *could* own) still lands
far better than a stretched or invented story, though at the Staff bar a thin answer on all of rows 11
to 20 is itself the signal that the level is a stretch.

| # | Situation type | Notes on which competencies it covers |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A technical decision you made under ambiguity, with incomplete information | Judgment, ownership |
| 2 | A conflict with a peer or your manager, and how you resolved it | Conflict, influence, emotional intelligence |
| 3 | A time you failed or made a significant mistake, and what changed afterward | Growth, accountability |
| 4 | Mentoring someone, or influencing a decision without formal authority | Leadership, influence |
| 5 | A time you pushed back on a decision you disagreed with | Disagree and commit, conviction |
| 6 | A cross-team or cross-functional project | Collaboration, communication |
| 7 | A time you had to scope down or say no to something | Prioritization, ownership |
| 8 | An incident, outage, or production issue you drove to resolution | Ownership under pressure, dive deep |
| 9 | A time you disagreed with data or metrics, or challenged an assumption | Technical judgment, rigor |
| 10 | Your proudest technical accomplishment | Impact, technical depth |
| 11 | Driving a technical change across multiple teams without authority over them | Scope, influence, org-level leadership |
| 12 | Owning a multi-quarter technical direction or strategy, not just a project | Vision, strategic judgment, ownership |
| 13 | A time you deliberately expanded the scope of a problem beyond what was asked, or identified work no one else saw | Initiative, ambiguity, scope |
| 14 | Raising the bar for others: a difficult teammate, an underperformer, or a team-wide quality/process change you led | People leadership, standards |
| 15 | Giving hard feedback to a peer or someone more senior, or receiving feedback that stung and acting on it | Intellectual humility, emotional intelligence, growth |
| 16 | Work that was not code but was critical: process, tooling, documentation, onboarding, or unblocking others through org friction | Glue work, organizational awareness |
| 17 | Navigating a disagreement between two teams or orgs where you had no authority over either | Cross-org influence, conflict at scale |
| 18 | Defining what problem to solve when it was not handed to you, or reshaping the roadmap rather than executing a given plan | Problem-finding, strategic judgment |
| 19 | Inheriting a project in trouble (late, over budget, unclear, or a broken team) and turning it around | Judgment under adversity, execution |
| 20 | A deliberate quality-versus-speed call: shipping with known debt, or holding the line on quality, and whether it proved right | Trade-off judgment, maturity |

For each story, fill in the following template.

```
### Story N: <short title>
Competencies: ownership, conflict, ...
Situation:
Task:
Action: (bullet the key decisions, not just events)
Result: (quantified where possible)
What I'd do differently, or what I learned:
Which questions this answers: ("Tell me about a conflict...", "Tell me about a failure...")
```

Keep the filled-in versions in a private `stories.md` alongside this file, ideally excluded from
version history if it contains anything sensitive; the table above serves as the index.

## Standard narrative pieces to pre-build

- **"Tell me about yourself,"** answered in 60 to 90 seconds, structured around where you are
  now, the through-line of your career, and why you're looking now. This should not be a
  chronological recital of your resume.
- **A project walkthrough**, roughly 90 seconds, covering the problem, your role, the key
  technical decision, and the outcome, for your proudest or most complex project. This often
  doubles as the opener for a system design round.
- **"Why this company, why now,"** answered genuinely, specifically, and with real research
  behind it. Avoid generic answers.
- **Questions to ask the interviewer.** Senior candidates are evaluated on the quality of these
  too. Have three or four ready per interview type, covering the team's technical challenges, how
  they measure success, what makes someone successful in the role at the senior level, and any
  recent technical decisions the team debated.

## Practice cadence

- In the first one to two weeks of your overall schedule, draft the story index and two or three
  full stories.
- On an ongoing basis, add or refine one story per week until you have eight to ten solid ones.
- Once you have a full bank, run at least three or four mock behavioural rounds (see [mocks](../mocks/README.md)).
  This is the only way to find out which stories don't land, or run too long, in practice.

## Anti-patterns to avoid

- Speaking in "we" the whole time. Interviewers need to know your specific contribution.
- Giving no result and no quantification. Always close the loop.
- Delivering an overly rehearsed, monotone answer. Rehearse the content, not the exact wording,
  so it stays natural.
- Picking stories that are too old or too junior for the level you're interviewing at. Prioritize
  recent, senior-scope examples.
- Never mentioning anything that went wrong. A good failure story, honestly told with real
  learning, is usually a stronger signal than a resume of pure wins.
