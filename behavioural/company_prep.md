# Company Prep, Logistics, and Negotiation

Everything in the other tracks makes you a stronger *candidate*. This track is about not losing
points that have nothing to do with your engineering ability: showing up unprepared on "why this
company," fumbling the logistics, asking weak questions, or leaving compensation on the table after
you've already earned the offer. None of this is hard; all of it is routinely under-done.

## Pre-interview company research

"Why this company, why now" is called out in the [resume and narrative guide](./resume_and_narrative.md) as an avoidable way to
lose points, but a good answer is a *research output*, not a talent. Before an onsite, spend a
focused block on:

- **What the company and team actually build**, and the problem it solves for its users (specific
  enough that you could explain it to another engineer).
- **Something recent and concrete:** an engineering blog post, a product launch, a public technical
  decision or talk. This is the raw material for both your "why now" answer and your questions.
- **The team's technical world:** the stack, the scale, and the kinds of problems they're likely
  working on, so your system-design and experience-deep-dive answers can speak their language.
- **How this role connects to your trajectory** specifically; an answer that could apply to any
  company is the failure mode.

Turn the research into two artifacts: a two- or three-sentence "why this company" you can say
naturally, and three or four genuine questions per interviewer type (see below).

## Questions to ask interviewers

Senior candidates are graded on the quality of these, not just their answers. Tailor by round, and
let them evolve as the loop progresses rather than repeating the same ones:

- **Hiring manager:** current technical challenges, how success is measured for this role, what
  growth from this level looks like in practice.
- **Peer engineer:** a recent technical decision the team debated and how it was resolved; what the
  hardest part of the codebase or system is.
- **Anyone:** what would make someone unsuccessful in this role; what they'd change about how the
  team works.

Avoid questions whose answers are on the company's public site; they read as unprepared rather than
curious.

## Logistics

Small things that cost real points when they go wrong:

- **Tooling.** Know the interview environment ahead of time: the shared coding tool, the whiteboard
  or diagramming tool for system design. Do one practice run in it so you're not fighting the
  UI live.
- **AI-tool policy.** Confirm whether AI assistance is allowed, since it varies: most live rounds
  ban it and some monitor for it, while a few take-homes now expect fluency with it. If you code with
  an assistant daily, do some practice with it off, so you are not leaning on autocomplete that will
  not be there in a live editor.
- **Environment.** Stable internet (and a backup, like a phone hotspot), a quiet space, camera and
  audio checked, editor/IDE set up if it's a take-home or shared session.
- **Timing.** Confirm time zones explicitly, build in buffer between back-to-back rounds, and know
  the full loop structure and who you're meeting so nothing is a surprise.
- **Follow-up.** A brief, specific thank-you to your recruiter or interviewers is fine and sometimes
  expected; clarify the timeline and next steps before you leave the loop.

## Offer evaluation and negotiation

A strong hire is undersold if the offer is mishandled. This is part of the outcome, not an
afterthought. The framework:

- **Timing.** Negotiate after you have an offer and, ideally, leverage (a competing offer or a
  strong current position). Get the offer in writing before hard negotiation.
- **Know the whole package.** Base, bonus, equity (and the vesting schedule, refresh policy, and,
  for private companies, the valuation and preference stack behind the number), sign-on, and level.
  Level is often the highest-leverage lever because it compounds over years.
- **Competing offers.** Frame them as fact, not threat, and let the strongest one anchor.
- **Non-salary levers.** Sign-on bonus, relocation, start date, team choice, and level are all
  negotiable and sometimes easier to move than base.
- **Know your walk-away.** Decide your floor and your alternatives before you're in the conversation,
  so you're negotiating from a decision rather than improvising under pressure.

This fits at the end of the process (Phase 4 into Phase 5 of the [schedule](../schedule/README.md)), but read it
*before* offers land, because the leverage-creating moves (running your loops close together,
lining up competing processes) happen earlier.

The most important of those moves is how you sequence the loops themselves, which is a deliberate
choice, not something to leave to chance:

- **Cluster the loops you care about** so their offers land within a week or two of each other. This
  is what creates the competing-offer leverage above; offers that arrive a month apart give you
  little, since the first one expires before the second exists.
- **Put a lower-stakes loop or two first.** The first real loop is where the rust comes off and the
  pacing settles, so it is better spent on a company you would accept but are not anchored to than on
  your top choice.
- **Leave enough space to fold in feedback.** Clustering fights against learning between loops, so
  the balance is a tight cluster of your top targets, with a short gap after the warm-up loop to fix
  whatever it exposed.
