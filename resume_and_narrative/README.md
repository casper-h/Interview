# Resume and Narrative Prep

This feeds two things directly: the behavioural round, and the project walkthrough that often
opens a system design round or a phone screen. It is worth doing deliberately rather than
improvising in the moment, since a rambling project summary is a weaker opening than a tight one
even when the underlying work is strong.

## Rewriting your project bullets

For each major project on your resume, rewrite the bullet in a form you could say out loud, not
just read.

- Lead with the problem or the business context, not the technology.
- State your specific role and decisions, not the team's collective output.
- Quantify the result wherever possible: a percentage, a dollar figure, a latency improvement, a
  number of users or requests affected.
- Keep it to one or two sentences. If it takes longer than that to say out loud, it is a sign the
  bullet is trying to cover too much, and it should either be split or trimmed.

Aim for six to eight of these across your recent roles. These double as raw material for your
behavioural story bank in `behavioural/README.md`, since a well-scoped project bullet often is the
seed of a full STAR story.

## The ninety-second project walkthrough

Pick your strongest or most complex recent project and prepare a walkthrough of roughly ninety
seconds, structured as:

1. The problem, in a sentence or two.
2. Your specific role.
3. The key technical decision or trade-off you made, and why.
4. The outcome, quantified if possible.

Rehearse this out loud until it flows without needing to check notes. This is frequently the
first substantive thing you say in an interview, and it sets the tone for everything after it.

## Tell me about yourself

Sixty to ninety seconds, structured around three things: where you are now, the through-line of
your career that explains how you got here, and why you are looking now. This should read as a
narrative with a point, not a chronological recitation of your resume.

## Why this company, why now

This needs to be specific and researched, not generic. Vague answers here are a common and
avoidable way to lose points that have nothing to do with technical ability. Before an interview,
spend time on:

- What the company or team actually builds, and what problem it solves for its users.
- Something specific and recent about the company, a product launch, an engineering blog post, a
  public technical decision, that you can reference concretely.
- Why this particular role, at this particular company, connects to your own trajectory, rather
  than a version of the answer that could apply to any company.

## Questions to ask the interviewer

Senior candidates are evaluated on the quality of the questions they ask, not only on their
answers. Prepare three or four per interview type, and let them evolve as the loop progresses
rather than repeating the same ones in every round.

- For a hiring manager or team lead: what are the team's current technical challenges, and how do
  they measure success for someone in this role at the senior level?
- For a peer engineer: what is a recent technical decision the team debated, and how was it
  resolved?
- For anyone: what does growth or promotion actually look like from this level, in practice?

Avoid questions whose answers are easily found on the company's public site; they read as
unprepared rather than genuinely curious.
